LIFT

A nutrition and training tracker that works offline and keeps your data on your phone. No accounts, no ads, no analytics, no subscription. Free and open source.

Pre-release. It works, but it's early — expect rough edges, and back up nothing you can't afford to lose yet.

What it does

Your numbers, in one place

Opens on today: calories and macros against your goal, what you've eaten, and what you trained. Below that, a week of stats — days logged, average calories, workouts, total volume — and charts showing how it's all trending.

Days you didn't log show as gaps rather than zeros, and the weekly average divides by days you actually tracked. Missing a day shouldn't look like eating less.

Steps, tracked automatically

Today's steps against a daily goal (10,000 to start, change it whenever you want), read straight from Health Connect. Nothing is written back, and the step count itself is never stored by the app — just read live each time you open the dashboard.

This one's native-only: browsers have no way to read Health Connect or HealthKit, so the web version only offers manual entry. If automatic step tracking matters to you, the app is where to get it.

The same calculator that's on this site

Mifflin-St Jeor, five activity levels, lose/maintain/gain, and selectable protein and fat targets — identical to the web calculator, so both give you the same answer. Save the result and everything else tracks against it.

Food logging that isn't tedious

  • Scan a barcode and the nutrition comes straight off the packet
  • Search by name against Open Food Facts
  • One tap to re-log anything you've eaten before
  • Grouped by meal, with calories per meal
  • Manual entry for anything the database doesn't have
  • Scroll back through previous days

Those three are a phone. The browser version is the same app on a laptop — one column still, just a wider one, so a whole session fits without scrolling:

Training that adapts to how you train

Every set can record weight, reps, RPE, time, and distance. Pick a focus — bodybuilding, powerlifting, CrossFit, Hyrox, or endurance — and you only see the fields that matter to you. Switching focus never loses anything: every set keeps every field it was given, the app just stops asking.

In the browser and the Android app the focus also decides which numbers you're shown. A bodybuilding session is summarised by volume and charts volume; a powerlifting one is summarised by its top set and charts estimated 1RM; Hyrox and endurance are summarised in distance and time and chart those instead. A new set starts at 10 reps under bodybuilding and 5 under powerlifting. The iPhone app still only changes which fields appear — the rest is coming to it.

  • Equipment tracked separately, so cable and machine pulldowns keep separate histories
  • Shows what you lifted last time, right under the exercise
  • New sets prefill from your last one
  • Save any workout as a routine and start it again next week
  • Per-exercise progression charts with estimated 1RM

Your data is yours

Everything you log stays on your device. There's no account to create, nothing syncs to a server, and there is no analytics or advertising code in the app at all.

The one exception is food lookup: searching or scanning sends that search term or barcode to Open Food Facts and nothing else. Your log, your weight, and your workouts are never transmitted. Enter foods by hand and the app never touches the network.

COOK

Recipes, a meal plan, and a shopping list that builds itself from the week you planned — then logs each meal in one tap when you cook it. It arrived in version 1.2 and it's in the 1.3 download above — the newest and least-tested part of the app. Tell me what breaks.

What COOK does

On iPhone

Use the web version. Open it in Safari, add it to your home screen, and it works offline like any other app.

That isn't a stopgap — it's where the beta is. Getting it in front of real people through the browser means fixes reach you the moment I push them, with no store review sitting between us. If you want to try LIFT and tell me what's wrong with it, that's the one to use.

There's a native iOS build too — the same tracker, the same calculator, the same COOK tab, reading steps from HealthKit instead of Health Connect. It's being built up alongside the beta rather than shipped from it. Once the app is solid, that's when it goes to Apple and Google. Not before, and no date on it.

Two things genuinely differ there, and both are in the food log. It has no barcode scanner. And its food search is the USDA library bundled inside the app rather than a lookup against Open Food Facts — so it finds fewer packaged products by brand, but it works on a plane, and it means the iOS app makes no network requests at all. Not fewer than the Android app. None.

The web version can't read HealthKit, so steps are manual there. Everything else is the same log.

On a watch

There are two watch apps — one for Wear OS, one for Apple Watch. Both do the same narrow job: log food on the watch itself, with no phone anywhere near it, and hand the log back to LIFT afterwards.

The food library is bundled inside the app — the same 7,793 USDA records the web version searches — so a watch that has never been paired to anything still has something to log from. Search it, set an amount, pick a meal. The watch keeps what you log: the most recent 200 entries, however old they are.

Getting it off the watch is the blunt part. The watch draws your log as a QR code — several of them, one after another, if you've logged a lot — and you scan them with Scan from watch in the web version. The camera reads the screen and that's the entire transfer; nothing is uploaded. Afterwards you clear the log on the watch yourself, behind a confirmation. The watch has no way of knowing whether a scan actually worked, and a clear that happened automatically would throw the log away every time one didn't.

On Wear OS that QR code is the only way off, deliberately. The Wearable Data Layer — the normal way a Wear watch talks to its phone — needs Google Play Services on both ends, and LIFT for Android carries none. Rather than add it, the watch app does without a phone entirely: it asks for no internet permission at all, so it could not send your log anywhere even if something in it wanted to. You search by voice or the watch keyboard, whichever your watch offers, and set the amount on the rotating bezel or crown in grams or ounces.

The Apple Watch app has been around longer and does more, because watchOS lets it: sets logged during a workout with a rest timer, and runs and hikes recorded with GPS and written to Apple Health. It also syncs to LIFT on a paired iPhone — the native iOS build, not the web version — but it installs and runs on its own, and the QR export is what a watch with no phone behind it uses.

The Apple Watch app: the main screen, a set going in mid-workout, and the log drawn as a QR code for the web version to read. That third one is the whole transfer — point a phone at the watch.

The Wear OS app you can now have: it is downloadable here as of today. Installing it is genuinely awkward, though — Wear OS has exactly one route onto a watch, the Play Store, and LIFT isn’t there yet — so it is a sideload over adb: a computer on the same Wi-Fi network, and about five minutes with a command line.

The Apple Watch build has no equivalent route at all. It has never been near App Store review, and it is signed with a free Apple account, which means it installs on my own phone and nowhere else. Same position as the native iOS build above: written, running on real hardware, not shipped. No date on it.

The iPhone "no" is the same one on the Coach page: the app is built, it runs, and it's signed with a free Apple account, which means nothing but my own phone will install it. It has never been to App Store review and there's no date on it. The browser is the iPhone answer today, and it's what everyone in the beta is using.

The plan row is new. As of LIFT 1.3 an Android phone opens a coach’s plan link in the app — it shows you what’s in the plan and writes nothing until you accept. The iPhone app has no way to receive a link at all, so an iPhone uses the browser. The COOK page goes through it properly.

Two of those rows are worth spelling out. A barcode in the browser is a number you type into the search box, not something the camera reads — the camera there is for scanning a watch, not a packet. And the iPhone app scans nothing because it has nothing to scan against: its food search is a database shipped inside the app, so unlike the other two it never touches the network. COOK's ingredient lookup in the browser is offline as well; it's the Food tab's search that goes out to Open Food Facts.

Coming

Three things that aren’t there yet, and what each one actually means — two of them are narrower than this list used to make them sound.

  • Editing a logged entry without deleting it and putting it back in. No version does this today — browser, Android or iPhone — and the usual reason to want it is a serving count that went in wrong
  • The exercise library on Android. The browser and the iPhone app already search the same 873 exercises by name and equipment; the Android app is the one that can’t. So this is a port, not a new feature
  • Ready-made splits — push/pull/legs, upper/lower, full body. All three versions already save your own routines and start a session from one. What’s missing is a set that’s there before you’ve written any

The splits wait on the library, at least on Android. A split is a list of exercise names, and if those names don’t match the ones the app searches, the history they generate lines up with nothing.
